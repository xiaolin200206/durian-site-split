With training length held constant, both levers helped by similar amounts: a doubling of farms and a doubling of images per farm raised unseen-farm mAP50 by {{regression/all runs/farms:.3f}} and {{regression/all runs/photos:.3f}}, and the data cannot separate the two. Two features of the design qualify any stronger reading. The steps in farms were uneven, with little change from two to four farms when all images per farm were used and a large gain at seven that the design cannot attribute to the number of farms alone; and at matched budgets more farms won twice and lost once. How training length is set also matters. In an earlier version of the experiment that capped training at 300 epochs, and so gave the smallest budgets far fewer iterations, the farm slope was {{v1_schedule/farms:.3f}}, as here, but the slope for images per farm was {{v1_schedule/photos:.3f}} rather than {{regression/all runs/photos:.3f}}, and the difference between them, {{v1_schedule/diff:.3f}} ({{v1_schedule/diff_ci/0:.3f}} to {{v1_schedule/diff_ci/1:.3f}}), would have favoured farms (Table S7). That schedule also scored higher in {{v1_schedule/cells_higher:w}} of the {{v1_schedule/cells:w}} budget cells, including cells in which both schedules gave about the same number of iterations, so the two differ in more than training length; the differences are listed with Table S7. Neither schedule is neutral, and the balance between the two levers depends in part on the choice. What distinguishes a farm is the classes it carries: a training set that lacks a class present on the target farm cannot detect it, and *Phomopsis* was photographed on three of eight farms. These results sit between two precedents. Beery et al. (2018) found that accuracy at new camera-trap locations was stable beyond two training locations; here, with all images per farm, the step from two to four farms was similarly small, and the rise at seven cannot be separated from which farms were included. Ruigrok et al. (2023) found for weed detection that, at a constant image count, more sub-datasets improved generalisation; the durian results agree only in part, since the advantage of more farms was not consistent across budgets. In practical terms, a first collection campaign should visit enough farms to cover the region's diseases and pests; beyond that, further images from farms already visited appear to be worth about as much as images from new farms.

### 4.4. Calibrating to a new farm

A device that meets a new farm could be adapted with a few images taken on arrival. Done by fine-tuning on those images alone, with the recipe tested, calibration made the detector worse: with 5 images mAP50 fell by {{rq3/ft/5/gain:|.3f}}, and even with about {{rq3/ft/all/images:.0f}} images it remained {{rq3/ft/all/gain:|.3f}} below the uncalibrated starting point of {{rq3/base_mAP50:.3f}}. The pattern of the loss, higher precision and lower recall concentrated in some classes, is what one would expect if a detector adjusted to a handful of images, which carry only some of the classes and few examples of each, learns to suppress what those images lack. Ruigrok et al. (2023) found the opposite for weed detection, where fine-tuning on as few as 25 images already reduced the generalisation error, with a different task and training setup. The fine-tuning recipe here was fixed in advance and not tuned; a lower learning rate, fewer steps or mixing the original training images into fine-tuning might avoid the loss, and this was not tested. Adding the same images to the training set and retraining avoided the loss, although with 5–10 images it made no measurable difference; only with about {{rq3/rt/all/images:.0f}} images did it raise mAP50 clearly, by {{rq3/rt/all/gain:|.3f}}. Retraining is not a like-for-like alternative: it costs a full training run and would be done off the device, after the images are returned. The practical conclusion is to treat a new farm's labelled images as additional training data rather than as a fine-tuning set on their own, and to collect a few tens of them rather than a handful. The calibration images were labelled by the author, so these results bound what calibration can achieve when a grower's images are labelled correctly; in practice that labelling is the cost.
